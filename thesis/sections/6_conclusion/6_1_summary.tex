\section{Summary}
\label{sec:6_1}

This research set out to investigate how graph-based data mining can be used to discover fraud patterns and develop predictive models for online real estate marketplaces. Using a dataset of over 800,000 listing events from the Swiss Marketplace Group, we built a heterogeneous graph with 31 million edges that encode relationships between users, devices, IP addresses, emails, and phone numbers. We then combined GNN-based pattern extraction with XGBoost classification and evaluated several different operational deployment strategies.

\paragraph{Pattern Discovery}
Our findings show that entity-sharing patterns provide a powerful signal for detecting fraud. Specifically, we found that "super-connector" devices associated with ten or more listings have a 35.5\% fraud rate—a significant increase over the 8.11\% baseline. We also identified geographic patterns, such as a high concentration of fraud originating from certain West African countries, and temporal peaks in activity between 6:00 and 7:00 AM, which suggest the use of automated attacks.

\paragraph{Model Comparison and Development}
We conducted a systematic comparison of four different model families and four GNN architectures, establishing a set of rigorous baselines that were previously missing from real estate fraud research.

While the aggregate AUC-PR for both the GNN+XGBoost and vanilla XGBoost models was 0.733, a deeper look at the results revealed a clear advantage for the graph-based approach. The GNN model caught 13 fraud cases that the vanilla model missed entirely. These "GNN-unique" cases typically involved massive fraud rings with an average of over 3,500 device connections, demonstrating that relational patterns can find what isolated features cannot.

Among the GNN architectures we tested, GraphSAGE emerged as the most effective choice. It achieved performance levels comparable to more complex models like HGT but was 22 times faster to train. This suggests that for the large, sparse graphs found in real estate marketplaces, simple aggregation methods can be more efficient than more complex attention mechanisms. Compared to the commercial SEON baseline, our framework improved AUC-PR by 17\%, showing the value of domain-specific graph mining.

\paragraph{Operational Validation}
We also identified several practical constraints for using these models. For instance, we found that graph-based patterns only begin to outperform tabular methods once the dataset reaches approximately 300,000 samples. Furthermore, our experiments with drift detection showed that a monthly retraining schedule offers the best balance between stability and model freshness.

Crucially, we observed that GNN-based models can be temporally unstable. Under a realistic "expanding window" evaluation, the GNN's performance dropped significantly compared to the vanilla XGBoost model. This indicates that real estate fraud often evolves by forming entirely new, disconnected graph components that the model cannot predict based on historical data. As a result, we recommend using vanilla XGBoost for real-time scoring, while reserving the GNN for offline analysis and the discovery of new fraud structures.

\paragraph{Interpretability and Deployment}
Finally, we used SHAP-based explainability to show that GNN embeddings account for about 17.3\% of the total feature importance. This decomposition gives fraud analysts a clear view of how much a listing's risk is tied to its individual attributes versus its network connections. With a proof-of-concept API achieving sub-100ms latency, we have shown that these models are not only accurate but also viable for production use.
