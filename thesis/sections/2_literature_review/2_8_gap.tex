\section{Research Gap and Thesis Positioning}
\label{sec:2_8}

While the preceding sections have covered the evolution of graph-based mining and fraud detection, it is clear that a significant gap exists in the current body of research. This section highlights that gap and explains how this thesis positions itself within the broader landscape of data mining literature.

\subsection{The Absence of Graph-Based Research in Real Estate Fraud}

Graph-based fraud detection has become a mature field with significant impact in both academia and industry. However, this progress seems to have entirely bypassed the real estate sector. A systematic search across major databases—including Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, and the ACM Digital Library—combining terms like "Graph Neural Network" or "network analysis" with "real estate fraud" or "rental scam" yielded no relevant results.

Major surveys of the field confirm this observation. For example, Cheng et al.'s \cite{cheng2024gnn} recent review of over 100 GNN-based fraud studies focuses on credit cards and money laundering, but makes no mention of real estate. Similarly, Mutemi and Bação's \cite{mutemi2024ecommerce} survey of e-commerce fraud addresses platforms like eBay and Amazon but excludes property marketplaces entirely. Even recent taxonomies of fraud domains, such as the one proposed by Compagnino et al. \cite{compagnino2025taxonomy}, leave real estate fraud out of their classification schemes.

The rapid progress in these other areas has been accelerated by standard benchmark datasets like YelpChi or Elliptic. No such equivalent exists for real estate fraud. Most existing academic work in this sector still relies on traditional, non-relational methods. For instance, Mohd Amin et al. \cite{mohdamin2024clustering} used K-means clustering on property listings in Malaysia, and older studies on Craigslist scams have primarily used heuristic rules or simple email matching. Even patents from major platforms like Zillow and Trulia focus on gradient boosting and support vector machines rather than graph-based architectures.

\subsection{Why This Gap Matters}

The absence of research in this area is a missed opportunity, as real estate marketplaces exhibit natural graph structures that are perfectly suited for network analysis. Relationships between users, agents, and listings—along with shared device fingerprints and IP patterns—create a rich environment for discovering fraud. The most common fraud modalities, such as phantom listings and rental advance fee scams, are often coordinated operations that leave behind relational traces that are invisible if one only analyzes listings in isolation.

The scale of the problem also justifies more attention. The FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center has reported real estate fraud losses exceeding \$145 million annually in the United States \cite{fbi2023ic3}, and investigative reports in Europe have highlighted persistent "gaping holes" in the fraud prevention systems of major platforms.

\subsection{Thesis Positioning}

This thesis addresses these gaps by providing what we believe is the first application of graph-based pattern discovery to the real estate fraud domain. We contribute to the field in three main ways:

\paragraph{Addressing the Domain Gap}
We use production data from the Swiss Marketplace Group to construct heterogeneous entity-sharing graphs. This allows us to characterize the topological properties of fraud networks in a domain that has not been studied this way before. Our findings, such as the "heterophily paradox," challenge some of the fundamental assumptions in the current GNN fraud literature.

\paragraph{Addressing the Methodological Gap}
Through systematic experiments, we provide the first empirical quantification of the data requirements for this type of research. By establishing a threshold of approximately 300,000 samples, we offer practical guidance that is missing from previous studies.

\paragraph{Addressing the Temporal Validation Gap}
We evaluate the stability of our discovered patterns under realistic temporal drift scenarios. This challenges the conclusions that are often drawn from standard academic benchmarks, which typically use fixed-split evaluations that do not account for how fraud tactics evolve in a production environment.

By combining a rigorous empirical methodology with large-scale production data, this thesis aims to provide both domain-specific discoveries and broader methodological insights that will be useful to the entire fraud detection research community.
