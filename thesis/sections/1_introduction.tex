\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:1_1}

The growth of online marketplaces has fundamentally changed how people search for and secure housing. In Switzerland, platforms like Homegate and ImmoScout24both operated by the Swiss Marketplace Group (SMG)facilitate hundreds of thousands of real estate transactions every year. However, this digital shift has also created new opportunities for sophisticated fraud. In these complex data environments, fraudulent activity is often buried deep within massive networks of transactions and entity relationships.

Real estate fraud in these marketplaces takes many forms. It might involve "phantom listings" for properties that do not exist, identity theft schemes designed to harvest information from applicants, or coordinated rings that operate through large networks of fake accounts. These operations leave behind distinct traces in the datasuch as shared device fingerprints, clusters of IP addresses, and specific temporal patternsthat are almost impossible to see when looking at a single listing in isolation. Instead, they only become apparent through a more systematic approach to data mining.

Traditional fraud detection typically treats each listing as an independent observation. Rules-based systems and commercial services like SEON analyze individual transactions but often fail to exploit the relational structures connecting them. While these methods are effective at catching obvious red flagslike disposable emails or high-risk geographic locationsthey are generally unable to uncover the complex network patterns that separate organized fraud rings from legitimate property management activities.

Despite the fact that graph-based fraud detection is well-established in other fieldswith hundreds of publications on using Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for financial transactions \cite{cheng2024gnn} and massive production deployments at e-commerce giants like Taobao and JD.comthere is surprisingly little academic research applying these methods to real estate. A review of the current literature shows that while e-commerce, money laundering, and insurance fraud are all well-studied, property marketplaces are largely ignored. This gap is particularly notable given that real estate scams cause over \$145 million in annual losses \cite{fbi2023ic3} and that marketplace data is inherently relational, consisting of complex networks of agents, listings, and shared devices.

This thesis aims to address this gap by applying graph-based data mining to discover, characterize, and validate fraud patterns in the real estate sector. By building heterogeneous graphs from entity relationships, we aim to uncover the "relational signatures" of fraud that are invisible to standard analysis, while also exploring the unique structural properties of fraud in this previously unexamined domain.

\section{Research Objectives and Questions}
\label{sec:1_2}

The primary goal of this research is to discover and validate relational fraud patterns in real estate marketplace data using graph-based data mining. We aim to show that these patterns not only improve the accuracy of fraud detection but also provide actionable insights that can support manual investigations.

To achieve this goal, we have structured our research around four key questions:

\paragraph{RQ1: What relational fraud patterns exist in marketplace graphs, and do they provide predictive signal beyond individual listing attributes?}
This question focuses on the core tasks of pattern discovery and validation. By building heterogeneous graphs from entity relationships and extracting relational representations, we hope to identify the structural signatures that distinguish fraudulent activity from legitimate listings. We also compare graph-augmented models against traditional attribute-only models to see if these discovered patterns offer any genuine predictive lift.

\paragraph{RQ2: What entity-sharing patterns characterize fraud rings versus legitimate multi-listing users?}
Moving beyond aggregate detection, we examine the specific topological and behavioral signatures of coordinated fraud rings. This involves analyzing device-sharing networks, IP clustering, and the reuse of emails and phone numbers to see how the "hidden infrastructure" of a fraud operation differs from that of a legitimate property management firm.

\paragraph{RQ3: How do fraud patterns evolve temporally, and what data requirements enable effective pattern discovery?}
Fraud is rarely stationary; scammers constantly adapt their tactics to evade detection. We look at how these patterns change over time and, perhaps more importantly, identify the minimum scale of data needed for these graph-based patterns to become useful. Using an expanding window analysis, we quantify the data requirements necessary for effective pattern mining in this domain.

\paragraph{RQ4: What actionable fraud indicators emerge from pattern analysis that can support human investigation?}
For discovered patterns to be truly useful, they must translate into operational value. We investigate which featuresboth individual and relationalmost strongly indicate fraud. Our goal is to produce interpretable indicators that fraud analysts can use to prioritize their work and guide their investigations more effectively.

\section{Scope and Delimitations}
\label{sec:1_3}

This research is conducted as a case study using production data from the real estate platforms operated by the Swiss Marketplace Group (SMG).

\paragraph{In Scope}
Our work includes an exploratory analysis of the marketplace's data structure and fraud distributions, followed by the construction of heterogeneous entity-relationship graphs. We use Graph Neural Network (GNN) techniques for pattern extraction and validate their utility through predictive modeling. We also conduct temporal analyses and interpretability studies to identify actionable indicators.

\paragraph{Out of Scope}
We have focused on batch processing rather than real-time streaming analysis. Furthermore, we do not analyze unstructured content like listing text or images, nor do we attempt to transfer patterns across different types of marketplaces. Our graph representations are static rather than fully dynamic.

\paragraph{Delimitations}
The patterns identified in this thesis reflect the specific characteristics of the Swiss real estate market, and their generalization to other regions would require further validation. Additionally, our ground truth labels come from existing operational systems, which may contain some degree of noise. Finally, because we are working with proprietary data, we provide statistical summaries rather than raw datasets to ensure privacy and security.

\section{Research Contributions}
\label{sec:1_4}

This thesis contributes to both the academic literature on data mining and the practical field of fraud detection, particularly within the context of Swiss Marketplace Group.

\subsection*{Academic Contributions}

\paragraph{Discovery of the Heterophily Paradox in Real Estate Fraud Graphs}
We have found that real estate marketplace graphs exhibit a fraud topology that is essentially the inverse of what is often assumed in the literature. While many studies assume fraudsters form dense clusters that require specialized "camouflage-resistant" detection methods \cite{dou2020enhancing,liu2020alleviating}, our findings show that it is actually legitimate property managers who form the densest entity-sharing networks. In our dataset, devices with five or more listing connections had a fraud rate of only 5.3\%, whereas single-listing devices showed a rate of 16.7\%. This "heterophily paradox"where high connectivity is a signal of legitimacyhelps explain why standard GNN architectures often struggle in this domain and provides a new empirical foundation for understanding fraud in property marketplaces.

\paragraph{Establishment of Graph Density Thresholds for Pattern Mining}
Through a systematic expanding window analysis, we provide an empirical quantification of the data requirements for graph-based fraud discovery. We found that graph mining only provides a predictive advantage over traditional tabular analysis once the training set exceeds roughly 300,000 samples. This appears to be the point at which fraud ring connectivity reaches a sufficient density to be distinguished from the noise of legitimate power users. This finding addresses a gap in the literature, which often demonstrates the utility of graph patterns without exploring the scale at which they actually emerge.

\paragraph{Demonstration of Temporal Robustness Inversion}
Our work reveals that the choice of evaluation methodology can fundamentally change the conclusions one draws about model performance. In a standard "fixed-split" evaluation typical of academic benchmarks, graph-augmented models outperformed tabular baselines by about 5.3\% in AUC-PR. However, in a more realistic "expanding window" evaluation that mimics a production environment with periodic retraining, this relationship inverted: tabular models outperformed the GNN by a factor of three. This suggests that fraud evolution occurs at the topological level, with new campaigns forming entirely new graph components. This discovery identifies two distinct operational regimes: forensic analysis, where GNN methods excel on large historical graphs, and real-time detection, where tabular methods offer far greater robustness.

\subsection*{Practical Contributions}

\paragraph{Complementary Detection Layers}
The patterns discovered in this research offer a secondary detection layer that can operate alongside existing systems. While SMG's current SEON-based tools focus on individual transactions, our graph-based approach identifies coordinated activities that are invisible to single-listing analysis. This allows for both "forward" detection (scoring new listings as they are submitted) and "backward" detection (identifying previously published fraud as new entity-sharing patterns emerge).

\paragraph{Empirically-Validated Rule Enhancements}
We have characterized several high-value patterns that can be used to enhance existing fraud rules. These include specific thresholds for "super-connector" devices (where 10+ listings per device indicates a fraud rate 4.4 times the baseline), cross-platform signatures, and specific temporal and geographic indicators. By providing precision metrics for each of these patterns, we enable platform operators to implement risk-calibrated rules rather than relying on simple heuristics.

\section{Thesis Organization}
\label{sec:1_5}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows:

\textbf{Chapter 2: Literature Review} surveys related work in graph-based pattern mining, anomaly detection in networks, fraud detection methodologies, and interpretable machine learning, positioning this research within the data mining literature.

\textbf{Chapter 3: Methodology} details the data mining approach including exploratory data analysis, graph construction methodology, pattern extraction techniques, validation experimental design, and temporal analysis protocols.

\textbf{Chapter 4: Implementation} describes technical realization including data pipelines, graph construction, pattern extraction via Graph Neural Networks, and pattern validation.

\textbf{Chapter 5: Results and Analysis} presents discovered patterns organized by research question: relational structures (RQ1), fraud ring characteristics (RQ2), temporal evolution and data requirements (RQ3), and actionable indicators (RQ4). The chapter concludes with interpretation of findings and practical recommendations.

\textbf{Chapter 6: Conclusion and Future Work} summarizes contributions, examines limitations, and identifies directions for extended pattern discovery research.
