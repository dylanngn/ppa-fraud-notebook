\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:1_1}

The proliferation of online marketplaces has fundamentally transformed how consumers search for and secure housing. In Switzerland alone, platforms such as Homegate and ImmoScout24---operated by Swiss Marketplace Group (SMG)---facilitate hundreds of thousands of real estate transactions annually. However, this digital transformation has simultaneously created fertile ground for sophisticated fraud schemes, generating complex data environments where fraudulent patterns are embedded within massive transaction graphs.

Real estate fraud in online marketplaces manifests in several forms: phantom listings advertising non-existent properties, identity theft schemes harvesting applicant information, and coordinated fraud rings operating networks of fake accounts. These fraud operations leave distinctive traces in marketplace data---shared device fingerprints, IP address clusters, temporal posting patterns, and entity relationship networks---that are invisible when analyzing listings in isolation but become apparent through systematic data mining.

The scale and complexity of this data presents both challenge and opportunity. Analysis of SMG's real estate platform reveals over 800,000 listing events forming a heterogeneous graph with 31 million entity relationships. Fraud patterns emerge at multiple levels: extreme entity-sharing networks (a single device connected to 6,251 listings), geographic clustering (West African origins showing 60--90\% fraud rates), and temporal signatures. These patterns, invisible in tabular analysis, become apparent through systematic graph-based data mining.

Traditional fraud detection approaches treat listings as independent observations, applying rule-based systems or commercial services such as SEON that analyze individual transactions without exploiting relational structure. While these approaches capture obvious fraud indicators (disposable emails, high-risk IP countries), they cannot discover the network patterns that distinguish fraud rings from legitimate property management operations. The gap between available data richness and analytical exploitation motivates this research.

This thesis applies data mining techniques to discover, characterize, and validate fraud patterns in real estate marketplace data. By constructing heterogeneous graphs from entity relationships and applying graph-based pattern extraction, we aim to uncover relational fraud signatures invisible to attribute-only analysis. Machine learning models serve as validation tools, confirming that discovered patterns provide predictive signal beyond individual listing characteristics.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:1_2}

Despite the richness of relational data in online marketplaces, systematic mining of fraud patterns in real estate platforms remains largely unexplored. Three interconnected problems define the research space:

\textbf{Problem 1: Undiscovered Relational Patterns.} Marketplace data contains dense networks of entity sharing---devices, IP addresses, emails, phone numbers---that potentially encode fraud ring structure. However, the characteristics distinguishing fraudulent entity-sharing networks (coordinated fake listing operations) from legitimate patterns (property managers with multiple listings) have not been systematically characterized. What graph structures, sharing densities, and temporal patterns differentiate these populations?

\textbf{Problem 2: Unknown Data Requirements.} Graph-based pattern discovery requires sufficient data density for meaningful structure to emerge. Practitioners lack guidance on minimum dataset sizes, graph connectivity thresholds, or temporal coverage required for relational patterns to provide signal beyond individual attributes. At what scale does graph mining become worthwhile?

\textbf{Problem 3: Pattern Actionability.} Discovered patterns have value only if they translate to operational fraud detection improvements and human-interpretable investigation guidance. How can mined patterns be validated for predictive utility and transformed into actionable indicators for fraud analysts?

\section{Research Objectives and Questions}
\label{sec:1_3}

The primary objective of this thesis is to discover, characterize, and validate relational fraud patterns in real estate marketplace data through graph-based data mining, demonstrating their utility for improved fraud detection and operational investigation support.

This objective is operationalized through four research questions:

\textbf{RQ1: What relational fraud patterns exist in marketplace graphs, and do they provide predictive signal beyond individual listing attributes?}

This question addresses pattern discovery and validation. By constructing heterogeneous graphs from entity relationships and extracting relational representations, we identify structural patterns distinguishing fraudulent from legitimate listings. Comparison between graph-augmented and attribute-only models validates whether discovered patterns carry predictive information.

\textbf{RQ2: What entity-sharing patterns characterize fraud rings versus legitimate multi-listing users?}

Beyond aggregate pattern detection, this question examines the specific topological and behavioral signatures of coordinated fraud operations. We analyze device-sharing networks, IP clustering, email/phone reuse patterns, and temporal coordination to characterize fraud ring structure versus legitimate property management behavior.

\textbf{RQ3: How do fraud patterns evolve temporally, and what data requirements enable effective pattern discovery?}

Fraud patterns are non-stationary; fraudsters adapt tactics over time. This question examines temporal evolution of fraud signatures and, critically, identifies the minimum data scale at which graph-based patterns emerge as predictive. Through expanding window analysis, we quantify data requirements for effective pattern mining.

\textbf{RQ4: What actionable fraud indicators emerge from pattern analysis that can support human investigation?}

Discovered patterns must translate to operational value. This question examines which features---both individual attributes and relational patterns---most strongly indicate fraud, producing interpretable indicators that fraud analysts can use to prioritize and guide investigations.

\section{Scope and Delimitations}
\label{sec:1_4}

This research is conducted as a case study utilizing production data from Swiss Marketplace Group's real estate platforms.

\textbf{In Scope:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Exploratory analysis of marketplace data structure and fraud distributions
    \item Construction of heterogeneous entity-relationship graphs from listing data
    \item Graph-based pattern extraction using Graph Neural Network techniques
    \item Validation of pattern utility through predictive modeling comparison
    \item Temporal pattern analysis through expanding window experiments
    \item Interpretability analysis to identify actionable fraud indicators
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Out of Scope:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Real-time streaming analysis (batch processing focus)
    \item Unstructured content mining (listing text, images)
    \item Cross-marketplace pattern transfer
    \item Fully dynamic graph representations
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Delimitations:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Patterns reflect Swiss real estate market characteristics; generalization requires validation
    \item Ground truth derives from operational flagging, which may contain labeling noise
    \item Proprietary data constraints limit reproducibility; statistical summaries provided
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Contributions}
\label{sec:1_5}

This thesis makes six principal contributions spanning pattern discovery, model development, and operational deployment:

\textbf{Contribution 1: Relational Pattern Discovery.}
We characterize entity-sharing topologies that distinguish fraud rings from legitimate operations. Analysis reveals that super-connector devices (10+ listings) exhibit 35.5\% fraud rates versus 8.11\% baseline, with the largest fraud ring spanning 6,251 listings through a single device fingerprint. Cross-platform posting (listings on both Homegate and ImmoScout24) indicates 26.74\% fraud rate---4$\times$ the baseline. Geographic clustering in West Africa (60--90\% fraud rates) and temporal signatures (6--7 AM peaks, February 2025 spike) provide additional high-precision indicators.

\textbf{Contribution 2: Hybrid GNN+XGBoost Framework.}
We design and implement a hybrid architecture combining Graph Neural Networks for relational pattern extraction with XGBoost for high-performance classification. Comparison across four GNN variants (GraphSAGE, HGT, CARE-GNN, PC-GNN) identifies GraphSAGE as optimal for this domain. The hybrid approach achieves 0.6990 AUC-PR---+5.3\% over tabular-only XGBoost and +17\% over the commercial SEON baseline---validating that extracted patterns carry genuine predictive signal.

\textbf{Contribution 3: Data Requirements Quantification.}
Through systematic expanding window analysis, we discover that graph-based pattern mining requires approximately 300,000 training samples to outperform attribute-only analysis. Below this threshold, relational patterns lack sufficient density for meaningful extraction. This finding provides actionable guidance for practitioners evaluating graph-based approaches.

\textbf{Contribution 4: Retraining Strategy Evaluation.}
We implement and evaluate concept drift adaptation through expanding and sliding window validation with DDM/ADWIN drift detection. Analysis across 12 months reveals that expanding windows outperform sliding windows (0.6286 vs. 0.5892 mean AUC-PR), DDM/ADWIN successfully detect drift events (March 2025), and monthly retraining provides optimal balance between freshness and stability. These findings inform operational retraining schedules.

\textbf{Contribution 5: Explainable AI Integration.}
We integrate SHAP-based interpretability to generate both global feature importance and local prediction explanations. Analysis quantifies GNN embedding contribution at 17.3\% of total feature importance and identifies screen resolution, payment mode, and listing category as top indicators. Local explanations decompose individual predictions into verifiable risk factors, enabling fraud analyst investigation prioritization.

\textbf{Contribution 6: Proof-of-Concept Deployment.}
We deploy the trained model as a RESTful API service with sub-100ms inference latency, demonstrating production viability. The service architecture supports both real-time scoring with graph-like count features and batch inference with full GNN embeddings.

\section{Thesis Organization}
